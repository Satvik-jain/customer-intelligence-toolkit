# Sources and provenance

This index links to sources checked for the public references. Sources identify established concepts, methodological guidance, or empirical studies; they do not validate this toolkit as a whole. Summaries below are brief and written for this project.

## Strategy and market frameworks

- **Five Forces:** Michael E. Porter, [“How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy”](https://hbr.org/1979/03/how-competitive-forces-shape-strategy), *Harvard Business Review* (1979), and [“The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy”](https://hbr.org/2008/01/the-five-competitive-forces-that-shape-strategy) (2008). Porter's articles are the primary authorial sources for the framework and its use in analyzing industry structure; the toolkit's question-to-framework table and cautions are project synthesis.
- **PESTLE:** [CIPD PESTLE analysis factsheet](https://www.cipd.org/uk/knowledge/factsheets/pestle-analysis-factsheet). Professional guidance for scanning external factors, not a claim about the framework's origin. The toolkit's selection and reporting workflow is project synthesis.
- **SWOT and TOWS:** [CIPD SWOT analysis factsheet](https://www.cipd.org/uk/knowledge/factsheets/swot-analysis-factsheet/) provides current professional guidance on using SWOT; it is not an origin claim. Heinz Weihrich, [“The TOWS Matrix—A Tool for Situational Analysis”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0024630182901200), *Long Range Planning* 15(2), 54–66 (1982), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0024-6301(82)90120-0), is a source for the TOWS matrix. The source set checked here does not establish a definitive origin for SWOT; no origin attribution is made.

## Segmentation and positioning

- **Market segmentation:** Wendell R. Smith, [“Product Differentiation and Market Segmentation as Alternative Marketing Strategies”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224295602100102), *Journal of Marketing* (1956). A foundational treatment of segmentation as a marketing strategy.
- **Segment evaluation criteria:** [OpenStax, Principles of Marketing, “Essential Factors in Effective Market Segmentation”](https://openstax.org/books/principles-marketing/pages/5-4-essential-factors-in-effective-market-segmentation). Educational source for common criteria such as measurability, substantiality, accessibility, differentiability, and actionability. The toolkit's additional scorecard criteria and its use are project synthesis, not a validated scale.
- **Positioning statement format:** [Harvard Business School Online, “How to Craft the Perfect Brand Positioning Statement”](https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/brand-positioning-statement). Practitioner guidance describing a target / competitive frame / value / proof format. The toolkit's wording is an operational template, not a canonical formula or a validated instrument.
- **Points of parity and difference:** Kevin Lane Keller, Brian Sternthal, and Alice Tybout, [“Three Questions You Need to Ask About Your Brand”](https://hbr.org/2002/09/three-questions-you-need-to-ask-about-your-brand), *Harvard Business Review* (2002). Practitioner article supporting the positioning checks for category frame, parity, and differentiation.

## Pricing and willingness to pay

- **Conjoint analysis:** Paul E. Green and V. Srinivasan, [“Conjoint Analysis in Consumer Research: Issues and Outlook”](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/5/2/103/1805825), *Journal of Consumer Research* 5(2) (1978). Review of conjoint use and methodological issues; the toolkit's method-selection prompts are project synthesis, not a complete study design.
- **Hypothetical versus real WTP:** Jonas Schmidt and Tammo H. A. Bijmolt, [“Accurately Measuring Willingness to Pay for Consumer Goods: A Meta-analysis of the Hypothetical Bias”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6), *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* 48 (2020). Supports caution that stated/hypothetical WTP and consequential behavior can differ. No universal correction factor is recommended here.
- **Comparative WTP:** Sharlene He, Eric T. Anderson, and Derek D. Rucker, [“Measuring Willingness to Pay: A Comparative Method of Valuation”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00222429231195564), *Journal of Marketing* 88(3) (2024; first published online 2023). Supports treating WTP as dependent on comparison and decision context; it does not provide a universal price rule.
- **BDM elicitation:** Gordon M. Becker, Morris H. DeGroot, and Jacob Marschak, [“Measuring Utility by a Single-Response Sequential Method”](https://doi.org/10.1002/bs.3830090304), *Behavioral Science* 9(3) (1964). Foundational source for the sequential elicitation method commonly called BDM. Applying an incentive-compatible design requires the study's payment and implementation rules to be specified; this reference is not an implementation protocol.

## Customer research and validation

- **Interviews and contextual research:** [Nielsen Norman Group, “User Interviews 101”](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-interviews/) and the UK Government Service Manual's guidance on [in-depth interviews](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews) and [contextual research and observation](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/contextual-research-and-observation). These are practitioner methods guides; qualitative findings help explain context, not estimate population prevalence by themselves.
- **Surveys:** [American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR), “Best Practices for Survey Research”](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/). Professional guidance on question design, sampling, collection, analysis, and reporting. The toolkit's short method-selection table does not replace these design details.
- **Usability studies:** [Nielsen Norman Group, “Usability Testing 101”](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/) and [“How to Conduct Usability Studies”](https://www.nngroup.com/reports/how-to-conduct-usability-studies/). Practitioner methodology guidance for observing people use products; usability findings do not by themselves establish demand or WTP.
- **Online controlled experiments:** Ron Kohavi and Roger Longbotham, [“Online Controlled Experiments and A/B Testing”](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4899-7687-1_891), *Encyclopedia of Machine Learning and Data Mining* (2017). Methodological overview for online experiments. The toolkit's method-selection table is a concise operational guide, not a substitute for a design, power, instrumentation, or analysis plan.

The market-intelligence reference also names strategic groups, benchmarking, and bottom-up TAM/SAM/SOM sizing as analysis prompts; the toolkit does not provide a detailed external method or benchmark for them. Case-specific sources, definitions, and assumptions are required.

## Consumer decision research

- **Heuristics under uncertainty:** Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, [“Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17835457/), *Science* 185(4157) (1974). Foundational empirical work for considering heuristics in judgment under uncertainty. It does not support inferring an individual's behavior from a label or demographic category. The broader decision-factor taxonomy in this toolkit is project synthesis.

## Synthetic customer simulation and evidence limits

- **Generative agents:** Joon Sung Park et al., [“Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior”](https://doi.org/10.1145/3586183.3606763), ACM UIST (2023). Demonstrates an architecture for interactive generative agents in a constructed environment; it is not evidence that simulated agents reproduce real customers or purchasing behavior.
- **Synthetic samples in a survey context:** William A. Argyle et al., [“Out of One, Many: Using Language Models to Simulate Human Samples”](https://doi.org/10.1017/PAN.2023.2), *Political Analysis*. Empirical findings are specific to the paper's survey tasks and population; they should not be generalized to consumer choice or market demand.
- **Retail user simulation benchmark:** Yada Pruksachatkun et al., [“CustomerSim: Benchmarking and Aligning Multimodal Language Models as Retail User Simulators”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08334), arXiv preprint (2026). Evaluates persona adherence and interaction behavior in constructed retail tasks. A benchmark result is specific to its models, personas, and tasks, not validation of a real customer population.
- **Persona-conditioned synthetic survey respondents:** Erika Elizabeth Taday Morocho et al., [“Assessing the Reliability of Persona-Conditioned LLMs as Synthetic Survey Respondents”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18462), arXiv preprint (2026). Reports that persona conditioning can redistribute errors and harm subgroup fidelity in the tested survey data; the study does not establish effects for every model or product domain.
- **Psychometric audit of synthetic respondents:** Mantas Lukauskas and Viktorija Šarkauskaitė, [“Plausible but Not Valid: A Psychometric Audit of LLMs as Synthetic Survey Respondents”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14606), arXiv preprint (2026). Tests whether synthetic responses preserve response structure and relationships in a specific dataset; its conclusion cautions against treating synthetic respondents as drop-in human survey data.

The three arXiv studies above are preprints, not represented here as peer-reviewed publications. Their findings motivate caution and evaluation; they do not prove that every simulation is inaccurate or useful.

## Project-authored operational synthesis

The following are toolkit-authored workflows and templates, not established or independently validated scales: framework routing and selection; segment scorecard; positioning statement and message checks; pricing-method prompts; validation method-selection table; product-fit signal families; channel test plan; evidence labels; persona schema; synthetic panel protocol; simulation evaluation checklist; orchestrator routing; and output templates. Use them as structured prompts for analysis, disclose assumptions, and validate decision-critical conclusions with appropriate evidence.
